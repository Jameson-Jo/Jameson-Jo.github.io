Queen Caline is a K class minimum diameter vehicle designed to go supersonic, so most of the engineering is about whether the components survive the conditions rather than whether the rocket gets off the pad. The airframe is Wildman G12 fiberglass, chosen as the standard material for minimum diameter builds at these speeds.

The nose cone is a von Karman profile, the Haack series solution at C equal to zero, optimized for minimum wave drag at fixed length and diameter rather than fixed volume. That constraint matters here: on a minimum diameter airframe the diameter is set by the motor, so length and diameter are the fixed quantities and volume is what falls out.

Fin flutter is the failure mode I am most concerned about above Mach 1, so I ran the flutter boundary by hand rather than trusting a simulation output. The hand calculation puts the flutter speed 69 percent above the predicted Mach 1.42 flight condition, a margin of 1.69. Recovery is dual deployment on an EasyMini with a Blue Raven as the redundant altimeter.

Performance 2 is sub minimum diameter, which means the motor is too large to sit inside a body tube at all. The motor casing is the same diameter as the airframe and carries flight loads as its own structure, joined to the rest of the vehicle through a threaded centering rod and coupler. That decision drives everything downstream, because the load path runs through the casing instead of around it.

My own technical work is on the booster: OpenRocket simulation of the flight profile, mass properties, and structural analysis feeding the frame, material, and shape of the booster body. We lean on OpenRocket heavily to gauge dynamic pressure through the transonic region and to bound fin flutter, since at Mach 3.3 the dynamic pressure peak is the sizing case.

I took the lead position in August 2026 and run a group of five other members. That means owning the timeline and the deadlines against a fixed launch date, and mentoring members through the work where they need it rather than reassigning around them.

On Encore I owned the avionics bay end to end: designing it, fitting the components, and testing them before flight. The bay had to fit a fixed spacing and a fixed available volume set by the rest of the airframe, so component selection and layout were driven by packaging constraints as much as by function.

Testing before flight was the part that mattered most. An avionics bay that fits and does not fire is worse than one that never got built, so every component was verified in the assembled stack rather than on the bench alone. The vehicle launched in May 2026.

Katana was an Apogee Components 54 mm motor kit flown on an AeroTech J250W-14A DMS motor to roughly 2,300 ft, carrying an onboard camera. Recovery was electronic dual deployment: I packed my own ejection charges and triggered them from a Blue Raven 2 flight computer, so the motor delay grain was not the deployment trigger and the altimeter had full authority over both events.

The vehicle was modeled in OpenRocket before the flight and reached altitude as intended. The recovery did not go as intended: the nose cone never separated, so the parachute never came out.

Failure analysis, three contributing causes

  1. The ejection charge was undersized for the volume it had to pressurize.
  2. The nose cone fit too tightly to the body tube, so the separation force required was higher than designed for.
  3. The vent hole sat too close to the ejection charge, bleeding pressure out of the bay at exactly the moment it needed to build.

Any one of the three alone might have been survivable. Together they removed the margin entirely. All three are packaging and sizing decisions rather than component failures, which is why the Queen Caline recovery bay is being laid out against them explicitly: charge size against the volume it has to pressurize, separation force at the nose cone joint, and vent placement relative to the charge.

ARCAS was my first high power vehicle, assembled from an Apogee Components kit with a 2.64 in body and a 29 mm motor mount. It flew to 1,500 ft on single deployment recovery and was the flight that earned my high power certification.

Because it was a kit rather than a clean sheet design, the engineering work was in the build and the flight readiness check rather than in simulation. I did not model it in OpenRocket. Stability was verified by locating the center of gravity against the center of pressure and confirming the margin before the vehicle went on the rail.
